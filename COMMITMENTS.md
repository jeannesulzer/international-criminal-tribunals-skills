# The Impact Litigation Lab's commitments

## Artificial intelligence and human rights

The Impact Litigation Lab develops open-source research tools for
international criminal justice. These commitments rest on a single
foundation: **international human rights law**. They respond in particular
to the concerns documented by Amnesty International in *Unlawful by Design:
Exposing the Human Rights Costs of Generative AI* (POL 40/0996/2026,
May 2026), whose analysis is itself anchored in international human rights
law.

### 1. We train no model and practise no mass data collection.

Amnesty International calls for the prohibition of standalone generative AI
systems built on unlawful web scraping — "the bulk and mass collection of
training data through the World Wide Web, without protection against
non-consensual collection of personal data". Our tools train nothing and
harvest nothing: they read, on request and one at a time, decisions that
the courts themselves have made public.

### 2. We work only on the public archives of justice.

The publicity of judicial proceedings is a fair-trial guarantee
(Article 14 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights),
and access to the archives of the tribunals falls within the right to seek
and receive information (Article 19). Confidential and sealed documents are
excluded by construction — never by exception.

### 3. We respect privacy as the courts themselves have adjudicated it.

Article 17 of the Covenant. We process no personal data beyond what the
tribunals have made public in their public redacted versions. The
protection of victims and witnesses is an absolute rule of our tools: never
identify a protected person; always prefer the public redacted version.

### 4. We refuse invention: a citation is verifiable or it does not exist.

The right to information presupposes accurate information. Our
verification-first methodology forbids citing case law from memory; when a
source is unreachable, our tools say so instead of filling the gap.

### 5. Our tools return documents, not verdicts — against automation bias.

Amnesty International documents the human tendency to favour a machine's
suggestion over one's own judgement. Our tools are designed against that
bias: they do not characterise, do not conclude, do not advise — they lead
back to the primary document and hand the judgement to the human. None of
our tools constitutes legal advice.

### 6. Openness exonerates us from nothing: responsibility lives in the design.

Amnesty International rightly observes that open-source availability does
not by itself amount to accountability, and that "usage policies" too often
shift responsibility onto the user. We take that criticism seriously. Our
safeguards are not instructions requested of the user; they are **built
into the design** — a tool that cannot cite without a verified source, and
that excludes confidential documents by construction. The openness of the
code and the methodology (CC BY 4.0) serves independent scrutiny; the
responsibility for design choices remains ours, and any reported error is
corrected publicly.

### 7. Our sole purpose: the fight against impunity and access to law.

These tools serve researchers, practitioners, human rights defenders, and
victims — never surveillance, never profiling, never decision-making about
persons.

---

*The findings, definitions, and recommendations referred to above were
verified against the primary document (POL 40/0996/2026, chapters 5.5, 6.5,
and 7). These commitments are a research-governance statement, not legal
advice.*
