# Methodology — Selection of Jurisdictions

**Impact Litigation Lab — Open Source Skills for International Criminal Law Research**

*Version 1.0 — June 2026*

---

## Why this document

This library encodes verification-first research methodologies for **16 international and internationalised criminal jurisdictions**, from the Nuremberg International Military Tribunal (1945) to Colombia's Jurisdicción Especial para la Paz (first sentences in September 2025). The selection of these 16 — and the deliberate exclusion of others — calls for explicit criteria. Without them, the project would be open to the legitimate question: *why these, and not others?*

This document sets out the five cumulative criteria applied for inclusion, situates the project within the existing doctrinal taxonomies of international criminal jurisdictions, and explains specific inclusion decisions that may invite scholarly discussion — notably the Jurisdicción Especial para la Paz (JEP), the Cour Pénale Spéciale de la République Centrafricaine (CPS-RCA), and the Chambres Africaines Extraordinaires (CAE).

---

## 1. Doctrinal landscape — existing taxonomies

Three principal taxonomies are used in the academic literature to classify international and internationalised criminal jurisdictions.

### Classical taxonomy (Cassese, Schabas, Werle, Ambos)

A three-category binary classification:

| Category | Structural criterion | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure international tribunals** | Created by multilateral treaty or Chapter VII Security Council resolution; international law as applicable substantive law; international staffing | IMT Nuremberg, IMTFE Tokyo, ICTY, ICTR, IRMCT, ICC |
| **Hybrid / internationalised tribunals** | Created by bilateral UN-State agreement OR national law with substantial international components; mixed applicable law; mixed staffing | ECCC, SCSL/RSCSL, STL, KSC, Special Panels Timor-Leste, Reg. 64 Kosovo, EAC Habré |
| **National jurisdictions with competence over international crimes** | Created by domestic law; primarily national staffing; national criminal law applied alongside international references | WCC-BiH (after 2009), CPS-RCA, JEP, national universal-jurisdiction prosecutions |

### Graded-hybridity taxonomy (Romano, Nouwen, Stahn)

This is the taxonomy most useful for this project. It recognises that **hybridity is a spectrum**, not a binary category:

```
More international ────────────────────────────► More national

ICTY/ICTR ─ ICC ─ STL ─ KSC ─ ECCC ─ SCSL ─ EAC Habré ─ WCC-BiH ─ CPS-RCA ─ JEP
```

Sarah Nouwen (*Complementarity in the Line of Fire*, Cambridge UP 2013) and Carsten Stahn (*A Critical Introduction to International Criminal Law*, Cambridge UP 2019) propose **five evaluative criteria** for classifying any given jurisdiction:

1. **Legal basis** (multilateral treaty, Chapter VII resolution, bilateral UN-State agreement, national law)
2. **Applicable law** (international, national, mixed)
3. **Composition** (international, national, mixed)
4. **Location** (extra-territorial, on the territory of the State concerned)
5. **Trigger mechanism** (Chapter VII, State consent, statutory reference)

### Functional taxonomy (Labuda, Van Schaack, Kersten)

A more recent taxonomy, focused on **transitional-justice function** rather than structural form:

- Retributive justice with conventional sentencing (most international tribunals)
- Mixed retributive/restorative models (ECCC's Civil Parties; SCSL's reparation considerations)
- **Restorative justice for international crimes** (the JEP's *sanciones propias* model — the most significant transitional-justice institutional innovation since 2016)

This taxonomy is particularly important for justifying the inclusion of the JEP in any serious mapping of contemporary international criminal jurisdictions.

## 2. The five criteria for inclusion in this library

A jurisdiction is included in this library only if it satisfies **all five** of the following cumulative criteria.

### Criterion 1 — Jurisdiction over international crimes

The jurisdiction has competence over **crimes of international law** — crimes against humanity, genocide, war crimes, aggression — or over crimes defined by reference to these categories (for example, the taking of hostages as a crime against humanity at the JEP).

**This criterion excludes** ordinary national criminal courts without competence over international crimes.

**This criterion includes** national jurisdictions exercising universal, territorial, or active/passive personality jurisdiction over international crimes — provided the other criteria are met.

### Criterion 2 — Structuring international or internationalised element

At least one of the following must be present:

- **International legal basis** — multilateral treaty, Chapter VII Security Council resolution, bilateral UN-State or UA-State agreement, or international endorsement (such as a Security Council resolution endorsing a national process)
- **International component in the composition** — international judges, prosecutors, or experts participating in the institutional structure
- **Substantial application of international law** — Rome Statute, the four Geneva Conventions and Additional Protocols, customary international law, international human rights law

**This criterion includes the JEP** on three converging grounds: the Colombian peace process is anchored in the UN Security Council — Resolution 2261 (25 January 2016) established the United Nations Mission in Colombia to monitor the ceasefire, and the implementation of the *Acuerdo Final* of 24 November 2016, including the JEP, is verified by the UN Verification Mission in Colombia established by Resolution 2366 (2017); its normative framework substantially incorporates the Rome Statute (non-amnestiability of international crimes, command responsibility, modes of individual criminal responsibility); and international experts participate at various stages of its work.

**This criterion excludes** purely national prosecutions of international crimes with no international element of any kind — though such cases may be the subject of dedicated future skills (notably the universal-jurisdiction practice of France, Germany, and other States, where structuring international elements arise from the substantive law applied rather than from institutional composition).

### Criterion 3 — Temporally and materially limited competence

The jurisdiction has competence over a **specific conflict, regime, or set of events** — limited in time and material scope. It is not an ordinary criminal court applying its general competence.

**This criterion includes** every jurisdiction covered by this library.

**This criterion excludes** ordinary anti-terrorism poles or specialised criminal divisions exercising general competence.

### Criterion 4 — Structured public documentation

The jurisdiction publishes its **judgments, decisions, and indictments** in an **official website** or in a **recognised institutional archive** (such as the ICC Legal Tools Database, the Avalon Project for Nuremberg, the IRMCT Documents Database for ICTY/ICTR, the Yale Avalon Project for IMTFE primary documents).

This criterion is operative: it is what makes the verification-first methodology possible. Without structured public documentation, no skill can encode a verification workflow.

**This criterion includes** every jurisdiction covered by this library.

**Notes:** the Special Panels for Serious Crimes in Timor-Leste and the UNMIK Regulation 64 Panels in Kosovo present greater archival challenges than other jurisdictions; they are nonetheless included because their documentation, while distributed across multiple repositories, is sufficiently structured to support verification.

### Criterion 5 — Substantial doctrinal contribution to international criminal law

The jurisdiction has produced — or is in the process of producing — **jurisprudence of international significance**: a doctrinal innovation, a precedent cited by other jurisdictions, or an institutional experience of broader relevance to the field.

## 4. Specific inclusion discussions

### 4.1 The Jurisdicción Especial para la Paz (JEP)

The JEP is, on the structural taxonomy, the **most national** of the 16 jurisdictions in this library. Its judges are Colombian; its applicable substantive law is largely Colombian (with substantial incorporation of the Rome Statute and international human rights and humanitarian law); its seat is Bogotá. Some scholars would situate it outside the field of international criminal justice *stricto sensu*.

Three considerations justify its inclusion:

**First,** the JEP is embedded in an international architecture built by the UN Security Council around the Colombian peace process in two successive steps: (i) Resolution 2261 of 25 January 2016 (adopted unanimously) welcomed the peace process and established the United Nations Mission in Colombia to monitor the bilateral ceasefire and the laying down of arms; and (ii) following the signature of the *Acuerdo Final* on 24 November 2016, the implementation of the agreement — including the JEP's sanctions and the *régimen de condicionalidad* — is verified by the UN Verification Mission in Colombia, established by Resolution 2366 (2017), which reports to the Security Council. This is not the structural hybridity of the ECCC or the KSC, but it is a substantial international anchor.

**Second,** the JEP's normative framework substantially incorporates the Rome Statute and international human rights and humanitarian law. The non-amnestiability of war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide, hostage-taking, torture, forced disappearance, sexual violence and recruitment of children is grounded in international obligations. Command responsibility, applied in the JEP's first sentences of September 2025, is derived directly from Article 28 of the Rome Statute and from customary international law.

**Third — and most importantly — the JEP constitutes the most significant institutional innovation in transitional justice since 2016**. The *sanciones propias* model, applied to non-amnestiable crimes for *comparecientes* who fully recognise responsibility, is a genuinely novel institutional experiment. Its first concrete applications, in the sentences of 16 and 18 September 2025 (Caso 01, Caso 03 Subcaso Costa Caribe), constitute jurisprudence of immediate international relevance — closely studied across the field of transitional justice. To exclude the JEP from a serious mapping of contemporary international criminal jurisdictions would be to ignore one of its most important contemporary developments.

The functional taxonomy (Labuda, Van Schaack, Kersten) provides the doctrinal framework for this inclusion: the JEP is the contemporary exemplar of restorative justice applied to international crimes.

### 4.2 The Cour Pénale Spéciale de la République Centrafricaine (CPS-RCA)

The CPS-RCA is institutionally hybrid in a manner closer to the classical model: 13 national magistrates and 12 international magistrates; principal financing and security through MINUSCA; the Loi organique of 3 June 2015 creates the court within the Central African national judicial system. The Affaire Paoua (judgment of 31 October 2022; appeal judgment of 20 July 2023) establishes its first jurisprudence, including a landmark application of command responsibility for sexual violence in an ongoing armed conflict.

The CPS-RCA's inclusion is uncontroversial under all five criteria.

### 4.3 The Chambres Africaines Extraordinaires (EAC Habré)

The EAC, established by AU-Senegal Agreement of 22 August 2012, prosecuted former Chadian President Hissène Habré before Senegalese courts under African Union endorsement. The Habré trial judgment of 30 May 2016 and the appeal judgment of 27 April 2017 constitute the **first African Union prosecution of an African Head of State for crimes under international law**. This is a foundational moment for the African Union's role in international criminal justice.

The EAC's inclusion is uncontroversial under all five criteria.

### 4.4 Jurisdictions deliberately not included (at this stage)

The following jurisdictions are not currently covered by this library, for reasons that the criteria above make explicit:

**National universal-jurisdiction prosecutions** (notably in France, Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain). These present a structurally different challenge: while they involve substantive application of international law, their institutional framework is that of ordinary national courts. They warrant dedicated skills designed around national procedural specificities. A skill on universal jurisdiction in France is currently in development in collaboration with Zacharie Laik (Legal Data Hunter).

**Truth and Reconciliation Commissions** (South Africa, Sierra Leone, Peru, Colombia's CEV, Timor-Leste's CAVR, etc.). These are not criminal jurisdictions; they are extrajudicial mechanisms. They are referenced in the library where they articulate with criminal jurisdictions (notably the Colombian CEV in the JEP skill) but are not themselves the subject of dedicated skills.

**Iraqi High Tribunal / Supreme Iraqi Criminal Tribunal**. The Iraqi High Tribunal raises particular questions of legitimacy and procedural standards that fall outside the scope of this library at this stage.

**Ad hoc claims commissions** (UNCC, EECC, etc.). These are not criminal jurisdictions.

**The proposed International Tribunal for Russia, the Ukraine Special Tribunal, the Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine** and other proposed jurisdictions. These will be added when they enter operational existence.

## 5. Updating the criteria

These criteria are the version 1.0 statement of the project's methodology. They are open to revision in the light of doctrinal developments and user feedback. Two types of revision are anticipated:

- **Expansion of scope.** Future versions of the library may include skills on national universal-jurisdiction practices, individual State implementations of complementarity, ad hoc claims commissions, and proposed tribunals as they come into existence.

- **Refinement of criteria.** As the field evolves — particularly with the development of restorative-justice and reparative models in transitional justice — the criteria may be refined to better capture the institutional diversity of contemporary international criminal justice.

Versioning of this document is tracked in `CHANGELOG.md` at the repository root.
